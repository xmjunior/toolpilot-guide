How many newsletter subscribers do you need to break even?

A formula, worked examples and scenarios for finding your break-even audience without confusing assumptions with actual revenue.

Reviewed November 8, 2026

There is no universal audience threshold. A small list with a relevant offer may cover costs that a larger list cannot. Your calculation depends on contribution per sale, the share of readers who buy, and recurring expenses.

Calculate my scenario

A worked break-even example

Required subscribers = monthly fixed costs ÷ (purchase rate × margin per purchase). Use the rate as a decimal: 1% means 0.01. Round up to a whole reader.

At $50 in costs, $20 margin and a hypothetical 1% purchase rate, break-even is 250 readers: 50 ÷ (0.01 × 20). With 1,000 readers, the scenario gives 10 purchases, $200 contribution and $150 after those fixed costs. These are assumptions, not observed sales.

These rates are not industry benchmarks. They illustrate sensitivity to one assumption. Zero purchases or zero contribution cannot cover positive costs.

Enter margin, not the selling price

If a product sells for $30 and delivery costs $12, the initial contribution is $18 before other variable expenses. Deduct commissions, expected refunds and per-sale expenses before entering your margin. Avoid counting an expense twice.

Count the work people often leave out

Add software, design, contributors and acquisition costs. To value your time, multiply monthly hours by your chosen hourly rate. Run another scenario without it to distinguish cash expenditure from compensation for your work.

Readers are not paying subscribers

This calculator uses total email readers and a monthly purchase rate. That rate is neither an open rate nor a click rate. For recurring paid subscriptions and platform commissions, use the platform cost calculator.

Validate before increasing spending

  1. Define the reader and the problem you solve.
  2. Publish several useful editions and invite voluntary replies.
  3. Test a small offer and measure actual purchases.
  4. Replace assumptions with your data and recalculate.

The model does not forecast churn, seasonality, taxes or demand. Adjust costs and margin to explore alternatives, without treating the output as a forecast.
